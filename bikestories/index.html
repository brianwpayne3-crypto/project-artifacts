Product research · controlled AI experiments

bikestories.bike

Domain owned · research before implementation

BikeStories explores a simple idea: preserve the small memories attached to a bicycle while they are still easy to capture, then discover the larger stories that become visible only over time.

Artifacts → Memories → Stories → Chapters → Life of a Bike
Don't write the story. Just save the memory.

The bicycle is the anchor

A physical bicycle can connect years of rides, builds, trips, owners, places and ordinary moments. The bike provides continuity without requiring every artifact or memory to belong to one narrative.

Memory is the capture unit

The owner should not need to know the larger story yet. A few artifacts plus a rambling recollection can be enough to preserve a useful memory. Some memories may never need to become stories.

Artifacts are broader than photos

Source material can include photographs, receipts, brochures, PDFs, Excel or other build spreadsheets, parts lists, notes, screenshots, ride and route data, GPX files, maps, audio recollections and other owner-supplied evidence. Current experiments have been photo-heavy; mixed-artifact understanding remains to be tested.

AI as editor, not author

AI can organize evidence, remove repetition, identify candidate relationships and construct readable narratives. It should be conservative about motives, chronology, significance and meaning that the owner or artifacts did not establish.

A key product constraint

A pile of material does not have to become a story.

Several photos of the same bike may represent one event, several unrelated memories, or nothing coherent yet. BikeStories should preserve material first, propose relationships provisionally, and tell a story only when one is actually there.

Bike identity ≠ memory relationship ≠ story relationship.

Uncertainty is also valid source information. “I don't remember” is not necessarily a failed interaction. The system should preserve what an artifact shows, what the owner recalls, what the owner is uncertain about, what AI inferred, and what remains unknown rather than flattening all of those into facts.

A learning product

The clarification conversation is part of the evidence.

When the AI sees an ambiguity, asks the owner a question, and then changes its interpretation, that interaction should not disappear as disposable chat. It can become structured, provenance-bearing product data.

Input artifacts → initial interpretation → uncertainty → clarification → owner answer → revised interpretation → Memory / Story / no-story

For a particular bicycle, those answers create continuity: BikeStories can remember distinctions the owner has already established rather than repeatedly asking or silently reverting to an earlier assumption.

Across appropriately permissioned interactions, the patterns can also help BikeStories improve its behavior without requiring the underlying foundation model to be retrained. Relevant prior examples can help the system recognize common failure modes, choose higher-value questions, preserve uncertainty and become more conservative about unsupported relationships.

BikeStories should learn not only from what owners remember, but from how owner clarification changes what the AI thought it knew.

This reinforces another working principle: minimize information gaps, not question count. The goal is not a fixed interview. It is to get increasingly good at recognizing which question would materially change a memory boundary, chronology or story decision—and when no more questions are necessary.

Personal continuity and general product learning are separate uses of this information. Using an owner's prior answers to understand their own bicycle is part of the product experience; using interaction patterns to improve BikeStories for other people requires deliberate privacy, consent and data-minimization boundaries.

Research so far

Testing the assumptions before building the application

Voyager 001: explored turning a frozen set of real bicycle artifacts and owner context into a visual narrative. It exposed how easily plausible editorial language can invent meaning.

Voyager 002: tested constrained editorial composition rather than asking AI to invent page design from scratch.

Voyager 003: used blinded story inference. A visually convincing riding photo was incorrectly treated as the ending even though it predated later changes—a useful failure that helped separate artifact selection from narrative truth. It also produced a reusable lesson: visually plausible chronology is not established chronology.

Experiment 004: deliberately supplied unrelated or loosely related material from the same bicycle. The model largely resisted manufacturing one overarching story, while showing that visual similarity can suggest relationships but cannot reliably establish memory boundaries. Image similarity is not memory identity.

Experiment 005: moved archive discovery from a product hypothesis into a working technical experiment. An authenticated prototype can search a private Flickr archive around an Apple Photos capture timestamp, compare candidate images using their original dimensions, and use Flickr EXIF metadata as an additional clue when multiple candidates remain ambiguous. The experiment demonstrated a plausible path from lightweight metadata captured from a local photo to the corresponding source image in an owner-authorized archive—without requiring the owner to manually browse and pre-curate the archive first. It is still a matching prototype, not a general archive-understanding system.

Archive discovery

Search broadly; preserve selectively.

The archive-discovery idea is now being tested with working code rather than only described as a future possibility. A local photo can provide small pieces of identifying evidence—such as capture time and image dimensions—which can be used to narrow an authenticated Flickr search to candidate source photos. Candidate dimensions are verified against Flickr's available image sizes, and EXIF clues can be inspected when more than one candidate remains.

This matters because the product should not require an owner to remember which photograph matters, find it in a years-long archive, export it, and classify it before BikeStories can help. The emerging model is to let an owner authorize a collection, use available evidence to find plausible source material, and preserve only what contributes to a memory.

The current prototype is intentionally narrow. It establishes that cross-library photo matching can work under controlled conditions; it does not yet establish reliable large-scale archive search, automatic bicycle recognition, memory boundaries, or story formation.

Over time, accumulated memories may reveal coherent stories; stories may form chapters; and those chapters may become an evolving Life of a Bike. The higher the synthesis goes, the more important provenance becomes.

The long-term possibility is less about maintaining a bicycle database and more about preserving grounded pieces of a person's lived experience around an object that persisted through it.

Current status

Research before implementation

The product model and AI behavior are still being developed through controlled experiments rather than a production application. Research has now expanded from frozen, manually supplied artifact sets into owner-authorized archive discovery and source-photo matching. The harder questions—memory boundaries, uncertainty, provenance, mixed artifacts, adaptive clarification, accumulated product learning and story formation—remain the focus before broader implementation.
