BCBG Service Intake — Product & Information Architecture

A designer-facing map of a working bicycle-service application. The primary object is a customer-owned bicycle moving through an agreement about what work should be done, the work itself, and a safe handoff back to the customer.

Current state

A working end-to-end pilot already exists.

This is not a speculative product concept. The application has already been built and is being exercised against the real Bucks County Bike Garage service workflow.

It supports the lifecycle from customer intake and bicycle documentation through inspection, findings and customer approval, authorized work, final QC, customer handoff, payment and closeout.

This artifact exposes the product and information architecture of something that already works so the workflow and experience can be challenged and improved before the implementation becomes substantially more polished.

Product & information architecture

How the service job moves through the system

01

Intake

  • Customer identity
  • Bike details
  • Requested service
  • Intake photos
  • Initial authorization
02

Inspection

  • Mechanic evaluates bike
  • Findings documented
  • Existing service reviewed
  • Additional work proposed
03

Customer Decision

  • Finding communicated
  • Proposal snapshot
  • Approved or declined
04

Authorized Work

  • Intake-authorized work
  • Approved additional work
  • Service lines = work commitment
  • Mechanic completes work
05

Final QC

  • Final once-over
  • Test ride / Not required
  • Finished-bike photo
06

Ready for Customer

  • Customer handoff
  • Completed-work summary
  • Customer communication
07

Waiting for Pickup

  • Customer notified
  • Bike remains in shop custody
  • Pickup / delivery obligation
08

Payment → Closed

  • Payment recorded
  • Bike handed back
  • Repair history retained
At every stage: Where is the bicycle? Who owes the next action? What must happen before the job advances?

Questions for design critique

  • Does this lifecycle match the mechanic's mental model, or expose too much process?
  • What should remain continuously visible as the mechanic moves between stages?
  • Are Finding, Customer Decision, and Service Line understandable concepts—or implementation concepts leaking into the product?
  • Should the interface orient primarily around the physical bike, the customer, or the service job?
  • Which transitions need explicit confirmation, and which should feel nearly invisible?
  • What should be immediately obvious when reopening a bike after several days?

The working application repository and customer/private operational data are intentionally omitted from this public artifact.
