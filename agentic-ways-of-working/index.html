Working operating-model thesis

Agentic Ways of Working

Exploring how dramatically cheaper implementation and translation may change team structure, collaboration, planning, measurement and the organizational bottlenecks around software development.

When implementation and translation become dramatically cheaper, the scarce resources move toward understanding the problem, making good decisions, validating them in reality, and coordinating the organization around those decisions.
implementation speed → cadence → coordination → team boundaries → roles & decision authority → measures of effectiveness
Constraint shift

The business may become the bottleneck

Engineering can potentially move through multiple build/test/revise loops while the organization is still scheduling a meeting, gathering stakeholder input or deciding what it wants. Discovery, domain knowledge, prioritization, decision latency and validation can become scarcer than implementation capacity.

Cadence

The sprint may survive—but its job changes

A two-week period can remain valuable as protected time, but less as a prediction of coding output and more as an outcome/learning horizon. Inside it, a team may run many rapid build-and-learn loops while remaining protected from continuous replanning.

Team design

Protect the multidisciplinary team

Sequential Product → Design → Engineering → QA handoffs struggle when implementation reaches the next decision in hours. The useful unit may be an embedded team of business/product, design, engineering and relevant specialists with shared context and meaningful decision authority.

Collaboration

Agents can reduce translation cost between specialists

A designer should not need to become a Git/terminal expert to contribute to an agentic software workflow. Agents can help translate artifacts and context between design, engineering, data, product and operations while each specialist continues working through their actual expertise.

Coordination

Decision envelopes instead of constant interruption

Before execution, establish the outcome, constraints, principles and decision authority. During work, let the team decide when context is sufficient, make and record cheap reversible assumptions, and queue only consequential questions that truly require missing business knowledge.

Judgment

Cheap implementation makes judgment more valuable

It becomes cheap to build the wrong thing. Humans remain accountable for intent, problem selection, constraints, product/design judgment, consequential decisions, validation and outcomes while agents absorb increasing amounts of investigation, translation, implementation, testing and intermediate artifact production.

A changing sprint

From delivery container to protected outcome horizon

Old framing: Here is the engineering work we estimate we can complete during the next two weeks.

Possible new framing: Here is the problem/outcome this multidisciplinary team owns for this period. The team has protected execution time, decision authority inside agreed boundaries, and a small mechanism for obtaining consequential business decisions when necessary.

AI should reduce the cost of implementation without increasing the organizational cost of coordination.

If 10× engineering throughput creates 10× more meetings, approvals and synchronous questions, the organization did not become 10× faster. It moved the bottleneck.

The execution layer

The agentic team needs an operating system too.

The organizational thesis is only half of the problem. Hands-on agentic delivery has exposed a second layer: the human-and-agent system itself needs durable context, explicit authority boundaries, predictable validation and checkpoints that another agent can resume without reconstructing the project from scratch.

Context

Durable context beats bigger prompts

Architecture, product decisions, release procedures, environment facts, constraints and current state should live with the work. A new agent should be able to recover the operating context from the repository and issue history rather than depending on a long conversation or repeated rediscovery.

Human judgment

Checkpoints are part of the system

Human product review is not simply an interruption to autonomous execution. It is an intentional boundary where judgment is valuable. When human testing changes real state, the checkpoint should record what changed and leave behind an authoritative state the next agent can understand.

Safety

Standardize safeguards instead of reinventing them

Ad-hoc safety machinery can itself create failure, cost and delay. The goal is not fewer safeguards; it is reusable, tested safeguards and explicit stop conditions that become more reliable with use rather than being reconstructed around every release.

Validation

Rigor should be layered and proportional

Routine changes should receive cheap isolated validation. Migrations need production-realistic disposable tests. Production should have deterministic read-only smoke checks. Consequential experience changes still benefit from human Preview review. More speed should not mean applying maximum ceremony to every change.

Learning system

Every issue should improve the delivery system

A migration surprise, ambiguous acceptance test, failed handoff or unnecessary agent restart should become reusable context, tooling or process. The next issue should begin with a better system than the previous one rather than paying to rediscover the same lesson.

Reuse

Ways of working can become infrastructure

The emerging practices should be portable: a generic agentic delivery layer for grooming, implementation, validation, human review, release and checkpointing, plus a small project-specific overlay for architecture, environments, risks and product-specific acceptance rules.

At the organization level, cheaper implementation changes cadence and coordination. At the execution level, agents need a delivery system that preserves context, knows when humans matter, and learns from every completed piece of work.
Measurement

What should a high-performing agentic team be measured on?

Story points and raw software output become even weaker proxies for effectiveness when implementation capacity is highly variable and increasingly cheap. Candidate measures move toward flow, outcomes, learning, quality and coordination cost.

Flow / responsivenessObserved problem → deployed improvement; decision latency; deployment → meaningful feedback; blocked/waiting time.
Customer / business outcomesDid behavior, adoption, operations or the underlying business problem actually change?
Learning rateValidated loops, assumptions disproven, speed from evidence to changed direction, willingness to discard work.
Quality / sustainabilityDefects, incidents, avoidable rework, change failure, coherence and debt appropriate to maturity/risk.
Team experienceCoordination burden, waiting on decisions, cognitive load, sustained focus and ability for specialists to contribute naturally.
Outcome cycle timeHow quickly and reliably can the team turn a real customer/business observation into a validated improvement?
Being tested in practice

This isn't only a corporate-org thought experiment.

The ideas are emerging from hands-on agentic work: using agents across requirements, repository history, implementation, testing, migrations, release work and documentation; defining explicit human acceptance boundaries; turning production and handoff failures into reusable safeguards; experimenting with how specialists can contribute without adopting an engineering-shaped toolchain; and operating close enough to real users that rapid implementation immediately encounters actual workflow and business rules.

That makes the work two connected experiments: what happens to the organization when implementation gets cheaper, and what operating system allows humans and agents to deliver safely without giving the speed back through coordination, context reconstruction and ceremony?

Still open

Questions worth testing rather than pretending are solved

  • Which sprint ceremonies remain useful for reasons unrelated to coding speed?
  • How should decision latency be measured without creating another gameable management KPI?
  • How much autonomy is safe in regulated, high-risk or dependency-heavy environments?
  • How do teams prevent rapid implementation from producing design incoherence or technical debt faster than humans can perceive it?
  • Which artifacts remain necessary when agents can translate among artifacts cheaply?
  • How do managers distinguish productive learning/rework from careless churn?
  • What happens when the business cannot supply decisions or validation at the pace implementation can consume them?
  • How much of an effective agentic delivery system can be standardized across projects without turning the standard itself into process overhead?
